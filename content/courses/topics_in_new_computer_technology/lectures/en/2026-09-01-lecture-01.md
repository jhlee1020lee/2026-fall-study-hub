## 10-Minute Review

Reinforcement learning (RL) studies how an **agent** selects an **action** from a situation, receives feedback from an **environment**, and improves its behavior to maximize long-term cumulative reward. The defining picture is not a neural-network diagram but an interaction loop: observation/state flows to the agent, action flows to the environment, and reward plus the next observation returns to the agent. Because an action changes the situation in which the next decision will be made, RL is a framework for [[concepts/sequential-decision-making|sequential decision making]]. [STT 02:26-05:24] [M01 p.5-6]

The lecture used “reinforcement learning” in three related senses: a **problem** of sequential decision making, an **approach** that learns behavior from interaction or decision data, and a **field** that studies those problems and methods. The course spans imitation learning, model-free and model-based RL, online and offline RL, multi-task/meta RL, and advanced topics such as human feedback, hierarchy, reward learning, humanoids, and generative models. [STT 04:42-08:23] [M01 p.7-10]

The “Why RL?” argument begins where supervised learning becomes insufficient. A labeled dataset can teach a model to imitate known outputs, but many important problems have delayed consequences, no correct action label for every state, and objectives that are easier to evaluate than to demonstrate. RL can search for behavior through reward, sometimes moving beyond human demonstrations. The lecture connected this idea to AlphaGo, chip design, language-model post-training, coding agents, and especially robotics and control. [STT 17:46-26:23] [M01 p.18-31]

## Core Concepts

### The agent-environment loop

At time `t`, an agent receives an observation or state, selects action `a_t`, and affects the environment. The environment transitions and returns reward `r_t` plus the next observation. Repeating this loop produces a trajectory. The agent's objective concerns the accumulated consequences of behavior, not merely the immediate reward after one action. An observation is the information available to the agent; a state is often defined as a sufficient representation of the environment. The opening lecture used them together at an intuitive level, leaving the formal distinction for later. [STT 03:41-05:24] [M01 p.6-7]

### Sequential decision making

In a sequential problem, the result of one action changes later inputs and opportunities. A quadruped's current foot placement changes its pose, momentum, contact pattern, and feasible future motions. Treating every action as an independent classification output would ignore those downstream effects. A good policy must therefore account for how present decisions shape future reward and future choices. [STT 27:46-28:21] [M01 p.32]

### Reward and return

Reward is scalar feedback supplied by the environment after an outcome. It is not necessarily a label that names the correct action. The learning objective is commonly a return: an accumulation of present and future rewards. This creates the credit-assignment problem: when a later success or failure occurs, which earlier decisions deserve credit or blame? Reward design is also a specification problem: if the numeric objective omits an important preference, an optimizer may find a shortcut that scores well without producing the intended behavior.

### Supervised learning, imitation learning, and RL

Supervised learning minimizes error against labeled targets. [[concepts/imitation-learning|Imitation learning]] learns behavior from expert demonstrations and is naturally applicable to sequential tasks, but it may suffer when the learned policy reaches states absent from those demonstrations. [[concepts/reinforcement-learning|Reinforcement learning]] uses reward and interaction or logged decision data to improve behavior. These are not mutually exclusive camps: demonstrations can initialize a policy, and RL can subsequently optimize it against a task objective. [STT 20:00-23:44] [M01 p.20-27]

### Method families in this course

Model-free RL directly learns a policy or value-related quantities without using an explicit transition model for planning. Model-based RL learns or uses a model and can reason about possible future transitions. Online RL collects new data while learning; offline RL learns from a fixed dataset. Multi-task and meta-RL investigate reuse and adaptation across tasks. These labels answer different questions and can be combined rather than placed on one single line. [STT 06:22-08:23] [M01 p.7-10]

### Why RL?

RL becomes relevant when correct action labels are unavailable or expensive, current actions have delayed effects, an agent must actively interact, or a performance objective can be scored more easily than optimal behavior can be demonstrated. AlphaGo illustrates that reward-guided self-play can discover strategies outside a fixed human dataset. The lecture extended the same motivation to physical design, language-model post-training, generation, coding, locomotion, and manipulation. This does not imply that RL is always superior; it identifies problem structures for which reward and sequential optimization add a necessary tool. [STT 21:52-26:23] [M01 p.23-31]

## Detailed Explanation

### Formulate the problem before selecting an algorithm

An RL problem needs a precise interface. The following table previews the formulation that the next lecture will formalize.

| Component | Design question | Quadruped example |
|---|---|---|
| Observation/state | What information is available when choosing? | Joint angles, velocity, body orientation, terrain sensors |
| Action | What can the agent control? | Target joint positions or torques |
| Transition | How does the world change after an action? | Robot dynamics and contacts determine the next pose |
| Reward | Which outcomes are preferred? | Forward speed, balance, low energy, low impact |
| Horizon/termination | When does an episode stop? | Falling, time limit, or reaching a goal |

If a policy trains with information that will not be available at deployment, the observation is invalid for the actual task. If the reward omits safety or energy, maximizing it may produce a technically successful but undesirable gait. These formulation errors cannot be repaired simply by switching to a more sophisticated algorithm.

### Three fundamental sources of difficulty

First, feedback may be delayed. A bad action may look harmless until many steps later, and an immediately costly action may enable a larger future gain. Second, data depend on the policy. In online RL, changing the policy changes which states are visited and which experiences become available. Third, useful data may require exploration. A policy that never tries an unfamiliar region cannot learn whether that region contains better behavior.

These sources interact. Weak initial behavior may visit only a narrow part of the environment; the resulting data then reinforce the same narrow behavior. Exploration can break that loop, but in robotics it can damage hardware or create unsafe states. Simulators, constraints, demonstrations, offline datasets, and careful evaluation are therefore not incidental engineering details.

### Online and offline data

Online RL can deliberately collect data under the current policy and adapt to discoveries. Its costs include interaction time, safety risk, and nonstationary data. Offline RL avoids new environment interaction by using a fixed dataset, which is attractive when data collection is expensive. Its central danger is extrapolation: an action poorly represented in the dataset may receive an unreliable value estimate, and the learner cannot simply execute it to obtain immediate corrective evidence.

### What “deep” adds - and does not add

Deep RL uses neural networks to approximate policies, values, models, or representations. Neural networks make high-dimensional inputs such as images and sensors tractable, but they do not redefine the objective. Delayed reward, exploration, credit assignment, policy-dependent data, and safety remain. “Deep” increases representational capacity; it does not turn a sequential decision problem into ordinary supervised classification.

### A coordinate system for method names

| Axis | One choice | Another choice | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Use of a model | Model-free | Model-based | Directness and simplicity versus planning and potential sample efficiency |
| Data acquisition | Online | Offline | New interaction and adaptation versus fixed-data safety and coverage limits |
| Source of guidance | Imitation | Reward-driven RL | Fast expert initialization versus discovery beyond demonstrations |
| Task scope | Single-task | Multi-task/meta | Specialization versus transfer and adaptation |

The lecture introduced these names as the map of the course, not as a proof that one cell dominates another. A method can be model-based and offline, or combine imitation with online fine-tuning, because the axes ask different questions. [M01 p.7-10]

### Lecture-specific emphasis versus added background

The instructor explicitly emphasized RL's three meanings, the question “Why RL after supervised learning?”, the robotics/control orientation, and implementation-heavy homework. The discounted-return notation below, the term distribution shift, and the detailed credit-assignment discussion are standard bridges to later material. They make “cumulative reward” precise but were not formally derived in this overview lecture.

## Examples, Equations, and Code

### Interaction and return notation

```text
observation o_t
      ↓
agent / policy π
      ↓ action a_t
environment
      ↓ reward r_t, next observation o_(t+1)
```

A standard expression for long-term return is

```text
G_t = r_t + γ r_(t+1) + γ² r_(t+2) + ...
```

where `0 ≤ γ ≤ 1` is a discount factor controlling the relative weight of later rewards. The overview defined the goal as cumulative reward but did not derive this equation. It is included to connect the verbal definition to the notation used later.

### Supervised imitation versus reward-guided learning

```text
Supervised / imitation learning:
  observation → predict the action recorded from an expert

Reinforcement learning:
  observation → action → transition and reward
  use many steps of outcome to improve the policy
```

Imitation can provide an effective initial policy without dangerous random exploration. RL can then search for behavior that scores better than the demonstrations, provided that reward, exploration, and safety are handled carefully. [STT 20:00-23:44] [M01 p.20-27]

### Reward-design exercise: a fast walking robot

Consider an illustrative reward:

```text
r_t = +1.0 × forward_velocity
      -0.02 × energy_use
      -0.5 × body_tilt
      -5.0 × fall_indicator
```

Each term expresses an intention and creates a possible failure mode:

- Rewarding only forward velocity may encourage the robot to throw its body forward unstably.
- An excessive energy penalty may make standing still optimal.
- Penalizing tilt alone may ignore damaging foot impact or joint-limit violations.
- A fall penalty delivered only at termination makes it difficult to identify which earlier action caused the failure.

The reward is therefore an executable specification to test, not a one-line natural-language answer. The intended behavior must be evaluated after optimization, including behaviors the designer did not anticipate.

### One way to combine imitation and RL

```text
1. Collect expert trajectories.
2. Train an initial policy with behavior cloning.
3. Run the policy in an environment or simulator.
4. Evaluate trajectories with reward.
5. Update the policy with RL beyond the demonstration distribution.
```

Steps 1-2 give exploration a useful starting point. Steps 3-5 can improve behavior in states not represented by the expert data. In physical robotics, simulation, constraints, offline data, monitoring, and staged deployment may be necessary to keep exploration within acceptable risk.

### Classify three tasks

| Task | Likely starting point | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Recognize handwritten digits from labeled images | Supervised learning | Each image has a direct target label and no action changes the next image. |
| Copy an expert driver's actions from recorded demonstrations | Imitation learning | Demonstrations supply actions in a sequential setting. |
| Learn a robot gait whose actions alter future balance | RL, often with imitation/simulation | Delayed dynamics and long-term performance matter. |

The classification is not exclusive. A practical robot system may use supervised perception, imitation initialization, and RL control together.

## Misconceptions and STT Notes

- RL is not a greedy rule that always selects the action with the largest immediate reward. The target is long-term return.
- Reward is not the same as a supervised action label. It evaluates outcomes and may not identify which action was correct.
- Deep RL is not “a large neural network with no RL.” The sequential objective and data-generation loop remain essential.
- Imitation learning and RL are not disjoint. Demonstrations can initialize or constrain reward-guided learning.
- RL is not universally better than supervised learning. The appropriate method depends on problem structure, available data, interaction cost, and safety.
- Model-free does not mean “no understanding” or “no neural network”; it refers to not using an explicit environment model for planning.
- Offline RL is not simply supervised learning on logged actions, because it still aims to optimize return and must reason about actions not uniformly represented in the dataset.
- Several occurrences of “reinforcement learning” were misrecognized in the raw STT. They were corrected against the course title, definitions, and M01 p.5-10.
